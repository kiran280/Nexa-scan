MRI vs. CT Scan: Understanding the Key Differences

A clear, patient-friendly guide to two commonly used imaging examinations and why the right choice depends on your clinical needs.

N Nexa Scan Editorial Team Patient education October 2026 7 min read
Clear information for more confident conversations with your care team.
Back to all articles
Share this guide

Medical imaging helps healthcare professionals see structures inside the body and gather information that may support diagnosis and treatment planning. MRI and CT scans create these images in different ways, so the most appropriate examination depends on the clinical question your clinician needs to answer.

In brief

MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves. CT uses X-rays to create cross-sectional images. Neither test is universally better; the choice depends on the clinical situation.

What is the difference between MRI and CT?

MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) uses a strong magnetic field and radio waves to produce detailed images of many soft tissues. It is often useful for assessing structures such as the brain, spinal cord, joints, muscles and ligaments.

CT (computed tomography) uses X-rays taken from multiple angles and computer processing to build cross-sectional images. It can be useful when rapid imaging is needed or when clinicians need to evaluate areas such as the lungs, bones or internal organs.

How does your care team choose?

Your clinician considers the body area being examined, how quickly answers are needed, your medical history and the advantages and limitations of each technique. The imaging team can explain what will happen and help you understand any preparation instructions.

At a glance

MRI

Magnetic field and radio waves

Often provides detailed soft-tissue images; examinations may take longer.

CT Scan

X-rays and computer processing

Often fast and useful for many urgent or detailed internal assessments.

What should you tell the imaging team?

  • Tell staff about implanted devices, metal fragments or previous procedures before an MRI.
  • Share any possibility of pregnancy and relevant allergies or previous contrast reactions.
  • Follow the specific instructions given by your clinician or imaging center.
  • Ask how long the examination may take and whether contrast material is planned.
The best scan is the one that safely answers the clinical question your healthcare professional is asking.

What happens after your scan?

Images are reviewed and interpreted by an appropriately qualified clinician. Your referring healthcare professional explains the findings in the context of your symptoms and other information. If you are unsure about your results or next steps, ask the team who arranged your examination.

Important: This article is for general education and is not a diagnosis or a substitute for medical advice. Your clinician will advise which examination is appropriate for you.
